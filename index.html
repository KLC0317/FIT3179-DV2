Australia's food system, from paddock to plate

Australia grows far more food than it can possibly eat

Australia is one of the world's great farming countries. In 2024⁠–⁠25 it harvested almost 35 million tonnes of wheat, and sold most of it overseas. It is also the driest inhabited continent, and in 2023 more than one in eight households could not be sure of having enough food. This page follows the food from paddock to plate to show how those facts fit together.

01 · Grow: how much, and how reliably?

The harvest is huge, and it is never the same twice

Australian cropping runs on winter rain. When the rain comes, the country grows a huge surplus. When it fails, the wheat crop can halve in a single season.

Sixty-four years of harvests, and the droughts that cut them

Top: million tonnes (Mt) harvested each year. Below: each crop against its own normal, ochre short and teal surplus. Drag across the bottom strip to zoom; click a band to isolate a crop.

In each of the five drought years marked, wheat fell 45⁠–⁠54% below its normal, and most other winter crops fell with it. Canola went from 34,000 tonnes in 1970 to 6.4 million in 2024.

A wheat crop can halve, then more than double

Change on the previous year, 1962⁠–⁠2024. The box holds the middle half of years, the line is the typical year, and dots are rare extremes.

Wheat fell 58% in 2002, then rebounded 158% the following year. Sugar cane, grown on the wet tropical coast, swung by more than a fifth in only 4 of 63 years.

Wheat alone is worth almost half the crop

Local value in 2024⁠–⁠25, grouped by crop type: wheat in teal, canola in violet, every other crop in grey.

Wheat was worth $10.1 billion, three times as much as every pulse crop in the country put together.

02 · Move: where does it all go?

Sold to the world from a narrow crescent of farmland

Most of the wheat is shipped abroad within months, mostly to Asia. Almost all of it is grown in a thin crescent of farmland across the south and east.

Most of the wheat goes to Asia, and the biggest buyer is Indonesia

Routes from Australia to its twelve largest wheat markets in 2024, which are shaded. Arrows point to the buyer; line width shows tonnes. Hover over a route to isolate it.

The largest customer is not China but Indonesia, at 3.46 million tonnes, just ahead of China on 3.23 million. Indonesia grows no wheat, so it imports all it eats. Almost three-quarters of the wheat exported went to East and South-East Asia.

Victoria works its land hardest for wheat

Wheat tonnes per square kilometre: a rate, not a total, so large states do not look important merely for their size.

Victoria produced 18 tonnes per square kilometre in 2024⁠–⁠25, against Western Australia's 5. States in grey have no published figure.

…but New South Wales and Western Australia grow the most

The same harvest as totals: circle area shows tonnes, with New South Wales and Western Australia in teal. Change the season on either map and the other follows.

Western Australia sold three times as much wheat as Victoria. Neither map is wrong: one shows how hard the land is worked, the other how much is grown.

South Australia's harvest more than halved in two seasons

Wheat sold by each state in 2022⁠–⁠23 and 2024⁠–⁠25. Choose a state under the chart: ochre if it fell, teal if it grew.

South Australia fell from 6.8 to 2.9 million tonnes in a record-dry 2024, while Western Australia gave up only about a sixth of its crop.

A country of 27 million people cannot eat a 35-million-tonne wheat crop. The surplus is the point of the system, which is why a dry year in New South Wales is felt in Jakarta.

03 · Eat: what ends up on the plate?

Abundance on the paddock is not abundance on the plate

The 2023 National Nutrition and Physical Activity Survey asked Australians what they actually eat. The answer has little to do with how much the country grows, and some households still go without.

Fewer than 5 in 100 Australians eat enough vegetables

Each figure is one person in a hundred (2023). A teal figure ate the recommended daily serves of that food group; a grey one did not.

Not one of the four food groups is eaten in the recommended amount by even a third of Australians. Vegetables are the worst: just 4.3% eat the five or six serves a day the guidelines ask of adults.

A third of daily energy still comes from discretionary food

Share of daily energy from discretionary foods such as confectionery, soft drink, pastries and alcohol; the rest in grey. Two national surveys, people aged 2 and over.

The share fell from 35.4% to 31.3% in eleven years. The dietary guidelines say these foods are for “only sometimes and in small amounts”.

More than one in eight households faced food insecurity

Each house is one Australian household in a hundred (2023). Ochre houses experienced food insecurity.

13.2% of households were food insecure in 2023, while farm, fishery and forestry exports earned $73.3 billion. Growing enough food and everyone having enough are two different problems.

04 · Sustain: what does it cost?

All of it rests on water that may not arrive

Every tonne in this story was grown on the driest inhabited continent. Rain decides how big the harvest is, and agriculture uses most of the nation's water.

Water use follows last year's rain

Rainfall (top) and water used (bottom) each financial year. Hover over a year's rainfall to light up the water used the year after.

Two dry years in a row ended with the decade's lowest water use, 12,148 gigalitres in 2019⁠–⁠20. The year after the wettest year used the most, 17,223 gigalitres: much of the rain is used only after it has filled the dams that irrigation draws on.

The wheat harvest rises and falls with the rain

Each dot is one financial year, 2014⁠–⁠15 to 2023⁠–⁠24: rainfall across Australia against that season's wheat harvest.

The wettest year grew a record 41.2 million tonnes of wheat; the two driest grew 17.6 and 14.5 million. Rain alone tracks the harvest closely.

Agriculture's footprint is far bigger than its economy

Agriculture's share of six national totals. Natural resources in teal; the dashed line is its 2.2% share of the economy (GDP).

Agriculture uses 68.3% of the nation's water and 57.1% of its land, but makes up just 2.2% of GDP and 2.1% of jobs.

Conclusion

So why does a country this productive still have hungry households?

The two things are barely connected. Australia's food system is built to produce a surplus and sell it: 71% of what its farms produce leaves the country, and the food that stays is priced on the same world market as the food that goes. A record crop in Western Australia does not lower a grocery bill in western Sydney.

What the fourteen charts share is variability. The harvest halves and doubles; the rain arrives or it does not. A system that puts 68.3% of the nation's water into 2.2% of its economy does not guarantee anyone a meal. It makes a great many meals possible, mostly somewhere else.
