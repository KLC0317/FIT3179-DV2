Australia grows far more food
than it can possibly eat

This is one of the world's great farming countries. It harvests around 34 million tonnes of wheat in a good year and sells most of it overseas. It is also the driest inhabited continent on Earth, and in 2023 one in eight Australian households ran short of food. This is how those facts fit together.

The harvest is huge, and it is never the same twice

Australian cropping is built on winter rain. When the rain arrives the country produces a staggering surplus. When it does not, the harvest can halve in a single season.

Sixty-four years of grains and oilseeds

Tonnes harvested each year, stacked around a centre line: wheat, barley, canola and other grains.

Canola did not meaningfully exist here fifty years ago. Growers harvested 34,000 tonnes in 1970 and 6.4 million tonnes in 2024, turning a novelty into the country's third grain crop.

Drought shows up as ochre columns cutting through every rain-fed crop at once, in 1982, 1994, 2002, 2006 and 2019. Irrigated sugar cane sits almost unchanged through all five, which is what a secure water supply looks like.

How far a harvest can move in one year

Year-on-year changes since 1962. The bar covers the middle half of all years; dots are the rare extremes.

Wheat fell 58% in 2002 and then rose 158% in 2003. Sugar cane, grown under irrigation, almost never moves more than a fifth.

What the harvest is worth

Local value in 2024–25. Same crop colours as above: wheat, barley, canola, others.

Wheat alone was worth $10.1 billion, three times every pulse crop in the country put together.

Grown in a narrow crescent, sold to the world

Almost all of this wheat comes from a thin band of country along the south and east. Most of it leaves again within months.

Where the wheat goes

Great-circle routes to Australia's twelve largest wheat markets in 2024.

The largest single customer is not China. It is Indonesia, at 3.46 million tonnes, just ahead of China on 3.23 million. Nearly two-thirds of the trade stays inside Asia.

The same two seasons, state by state

Each line is one state between 2022–23 and 2024–25.

South Australia's harvest more than halved, from 6.8 to 2.9 million tonnes, while Western Australia gave up only about a sixth of its crop.

Why so much leaves

71%

of everything Australian agriculture produces is exported, averaged across 2022–23 to 2024–25.

A country of 27 million people cannot eat a 34 million tonne wheat crop. The surplus is the entire point of the system, and it is why a dry year in New South Wales is felt in Jakarta and Manila.

Abundance on the paddock is not abundance on the plate

The 2023 National Nutrition and Physical Activity Survey asked Australians what they actually ate. The answer has very little to do with how much the country grows.

How many of us eat the recommended amount

Each icon is one person in a hundred; a filled icon met the guideline.

Fewer than five people in a hundred eat the recommended vegetables, legumes and beans. Not one of the four food groups reaches even a third of the population.

Discretionary food is losing ground

Share of daily energy from confectionery, soft drink, pastries and alcohol.

Down 4.1 percentage points in eleven years, which is real progress. It still leaves roughly a third of everything eaten in the discretionary column.

And some households simply go without

Each house icon is one Australian household in a hundred.

13.2% of households ran short of food in the same year the country exported $75.8 billion of farm produce. Growing enough food and everyone having enough food are two different problems.

All of it rests on water that may not arrive

Every tonne in this story was grown with rainfall and irrigation on the driest inhabited continent. That is the constraint the whole system is built around.

Rain falls, water gets used

Each point is one financial year, joined in time order.

The path collapses to the bottom left in 2019–20, which tied 2018–19 as the driest year at 347 mm but used the least water of the decade by a wide margin, 12,148 gigalitres. By 2023–24 both had climbed back.

The trade-off in one number

31×

Agriculture takes 68.3% of Australia's water while producing 2.2% of its GDP.

That is not automatically a bad bargain. Food is not optional and export income is real. But it is the number every argument about Australian water policy eventually comes back to.

Its footprint against its economic weight

Each bar runs from agriculture's 2.2% share of GDP to its share of a different national total.

Agriculture uses 31 times more of the nation's water, and 26 times more of its land, than its share of the economy would suggest. Its share of jobs, by contrast, sits almost exactly on its share of GDP.

Ten years of the water account

Each line is one year across four measures, each axis on its own scale.

National water use swings with the rain, but household use slides quietly down the frame year after year: 187 kilolitres per household in 2014–15, 174 by 2023–24, regardless of how wet the year was.

So why does a country this productive still have hungry households?

Because the two things are barely connected. Australia's food system is built to produce a surplus and sell it: 71% of what it grows leaves the country, and the harvest that stays is priced on the same world market as the harvest that goes. A record crop in Western Australia does not lower anyone's grocery bill in western Sydney.

What the fourteen charts have in common is variability. The harvest halves and doubles; the rain arrives or it does not. A system with that much natural volatility, feeding 68.3% of the nation's water into 2.2% of its economy, does not guarantee anybody a meal. It just makes a great many of them possible, somewhere else.
