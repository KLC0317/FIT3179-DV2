Australia's food system, from paddock to plate

Australia grows far more food
than it can possibly eat

Australia is one of the world's great farming countries. In 2024⁠–⁠25 it harvested almost 35 million tonnes of wheat, and most of it was sold overseas. It is also the driest inhabited continent on Earth, and in 2023 more than one in eight households could not be sure of having enough food. This page follows the food from the paddock to the plate to show how those facts fit together.

01 · Grow: how much, and how reliably?

The harvest is huge, and it is never the same twice

Australian cropping runs on winter rain. When the rain comes, the country produces a staggering surplus. When it fails, the wheat crop can halve in a single season. Start with sixty-four years of harvests.

Sixty-four years of harvests: wheat dominates, canola arrives

Million tonnes (Mt) harvested each year, stacked around a centre line: wheat, barley, canola and other grains. Click a band to isolate it.

Canola barely existed here fifty years ago. Growers harvested 34,000 tonnes in 1970 and 6.4 million tonnes in 2024, making it the third-biggest crop on this chart, behind only wheat and barley.

Droughts cut through the winter crops all at once

Each cell is one crop in one year, compared with its own eleven-year normal: ochre is a short harvest, teal a surplus.

In each of the five drought years marked, wheat fell 45⁠–⁠54% below its normal, and most other winter crops fell with it. Irrigated sugar cane barely changes colour, which is what a secure water supply looks like.

A wheat crop can halve, then more than double

Change on the previous year, 1962⁠–⁠2024. The box holds the middle half of years, the line is the typical year, and dots are rare extremes.

Wheat fell 58% in 2002, then rose 158% in 2003. Sugar cane, grown under irrigation, moved by more than a fifth in only 4 of 63 years.

Wheat alone is worth almost half the crop

Local value in 2024⁠–⁠25, grouped by crop type. Same colours as above: wheat, barley, canola and others.

Wheat was worth $10.1 billion, three times as much as every pulse crop in the country put together.

02 · Move: where is it grown, and where does it go?

Grown in a narrow crescent, sold to the world

Almost all of that wheat comes from a thin band of country along the south and east of the continent. Most of it leaves again within months, and most of what leaves goes to Asia.

Victoria works its land hardest for wheat

Tonnes of wheat per square kilometre of each state. A rate, not a total, so big states do not look important just for being big.

Victoria produced 18 tonnes per square kilometre in 2024⁠–⁠25, against Western Australia's 5. The Northern Territory is grey because the Australian Bureau of Statistics published no figure for it.

…but New South Wales and Western Australia grow the most

The same harvest as totals: circle area shows tonnes, with New South Wales and Western Australia in teal. Change the season on either map and the other follows.

Western Australia sold three times as much wheat as Victoria in 2024⁠–⁠25. Neither map is wrong: one shows how hard the land is worked, the other how much is grown.

Most of the wheat goes to Asia, and the biggest buyer is Indonesia

Great-circle routes to Australia's twelve largest wheat markets in 2024, which are shaded. Arrows point to the buyer, and line width shows million tonnes (Mt). Hover over a route to isolate it.

The largest customer is not China but Indonesia, at 3.46 million tonnes, just ahead of China on 3.23 million. Almost three-quarters of Australia's wheat exports went to East and South-East Asia.

South Australia's harvest more than halved in two seasons

Wheat sold by each state in 2022⁠–⁠23 and 2024⁠–⁠25. South Australia is highlighted.

South Australia fell from 6.8 to 2.9 million tonnes, while Western Australia gave up only about a sixth of its crop. A national total would hide both stories.

Why so much of it leaves

What Australian farms produce, and what it earns overseas.

71%

of everything Australian farms produce is exported, by volume, averaged over 2022⁠–⁠23 to 2024⁠–⁠25.

$75.8 billion

earned from agriculture, fisheries and forestry exports in 2024⁠–⁠25.

A country of 27 million people cannot eat a 35-million-tonne wheat crop. The surplus is the point of the system, which is why a dry year in New South Wales is felt in Jakarta and Manila.

03 · Eat: what ends up on the plate?

Abundance on the paddock is not abundance on the plate

The 2023 National Nutrition and Physical Activity Survey asked Australians what they actually eat. The answer has very little to do with how much the country grows, and some households still go without.

Fewer than 5 in 100 Australians eat enough vegetables

Each figure is one person in a hundred (2023). A teal figure ate the recommended daily serves of that food group; a grey one did not.

Not one of the four food groups is eaten in the recommended amount by even a third of Australians. Vegetables are the worst: just 4.3% eat enough.

Discretionary food is losing ground, slowly

Share of daily energy from foods such as confectionery, soft drink, pastries and alcohol, for people aged 2 and over.

The share fell 4.1 percentage points in eleven years, which is real progress. It still leaves almost a third of all energy coming from discretionary food.

More than one in eight households faced food insecurity

Each house is one Australian household in a hundred (2023). Ochre houses experienced food insecurity.

In 2023, 13.2% of households were food insecure, while farm, fishery and forestry exports earned $73.3 billion in 2023⁠–⁠24. Growing enough food and everyone having enough food are two different problems.

04 · Sustain: what does it cost?

All of it rests on water that may not arrive

Every tonne in this story was grown with rain and irrigation on the driest inhabited continent. Water is the constraint the whole system is built around, and agriculture uses most of it.

Agriculture's footprint is far bigger than its economic weight

Each dumbbell runs from agriculture's 2.2% share of the economy (GDP) to its share of another national total. Natural resources are in teal.

Agriculture uses 31 times more of the nation's water, and 26 times more of its land, than its share of the economy would suggest, while its share of jobs sits almost exactly on its share of GDP. That is not automatically a bad bargain: food is not optional, and export income is real.

Water use follows last year's rain

Rainfall (top) and water used (bottom) each year. Ochre marks the driest years and the low that followed; teal, the wettest year and the peak that followed.

Two dry years in a row ended with the decade's lowest water use, 12,148 gigalitres in 2019⁠–⁠20. The year after the wettest year used the most, 17,223 gigalitres. The likely reason: much of the rain is used only after it has filled the dams that irrigation draws on.

Household water use drifts down, wet years or dry

Each line is one year across four measures, each axis running from its own ten-year low to high. Hover over a line to follow one year.

Total water use and water per dollar of output swing together from year to year, but household use has drifted down, from 187 kilolitres per household in 2014⁠–⁠15 to 174 in 2023⁠–⁠24.

Conclusion

So why does a country this productive still have hungry households?

The two things are barely connected. Australia's food system is built to produce a surplus and sell it: 71% of what its farms produce leaves the country, and the food that stays is priced on the same world market as the food that goes. A record crop in Western Australia does not lower anyone's grocery bill in western Sydney.

What the fourteen charts share is variability. The harvest halves and doubles; the rain arrives or it does not. A system that puts 68.3% of the nation's water into 2.2% of its economy, under that much natural volatility, does not guarantee anyone a meal. It makes a great many meals possible, mostly somewhere else.

  2. 02 · Move Australia grows for the world. Most of the wheat is exported, and almost three-quarters of that goes to East and South-East Asia.
  3. 03 · Eat Plenty of food is not the same as enough food. More than one household in eight was food insecure in 2023.
  4. 04 · Sustain It all runs on water. Agriculture uses 68.3% of the nation's water, and how much it uses follows the previous year's rain.
